# Negative prices

## Why prices go negative
Negative prices occur when inflexible generation exceeds demand plus export capacity. Typical contributors:
- High solar output around midday, especially on weekends and public holidays when demand is low.
- High wind output at night or on weekends.
- Must-run thermal plants (combined heat and power, system services) that cannot ramp down cheaply.
- Renewable plants paid through older support schemes that receive payments whether or not the price is negative, so they keep producing.

## Typical pattern in Germany
Negative hours cluster between roughly 10:00 and 16:00 on sunny days from April to August, most often on Sundays and public holidays. The deepest negative prices on record in DE-LU reach the harmonised floor of -500 EUR/MWh. A "duck curve" shape is typical: low or negative prices at midday followed by a steep ramp to high prices in the evening, when solar output disappears and residual load rises quickly.

## Regulatory context (Germany)
- Under the Renewable Energy Sources Act (EEG), plants in direct marketing lose their market premium during periods of negative prices. The threshold has been tightened over time.
- The "Solarspitzengesetz" (in force since February 2025) means new PV installations receive no feed-in remuneration during negative-price periods. It also limits feed-in for new small systems without a smart meter.
- Batteries and flexible demand earn money from negative prices (they charge) and from the evening ramp (they discharge). Growing storage capacity tends to flatten the midday price trough over time.

## What to report in a briefing
Number of negative hours, the minimum price and when it occurred, solar and wind output in those hours, whether the day was a weekend or holiday, and the net export position (exports usually peak during negative hours).
